26 in 26 :) GATE EE: How I Secured AIR 26 in 2026

Created on March 29, 2026

2026   ·   gate   electrical-engineering   preparation-guide   engineering   ·   Engineering   Mentorship

A few years ago, I wrote about securing AIR 2 in GATE Electronics and Communication Engineering in my third year at IIT Bombay.

This time, the story is somewhat different.

In 2026, I appeared for GATE Electrical Engineering and secured AIR 26.

What makes this result interesting to me is not simply the rank. It is the fact that EE was not something in which I had the strongest preparation. Second, I had no intentions to give the exam but since I had filled it, I thought to go and attempt whatever it goes.

As I always prefer, practice practice practice, no other way to become “marks intelligent”. NPTEL is a good source as mentioned in the other article. Rest of the below is AI & not that important.

From Electronics to Electrical

My preparation background has always been much closer to Electronics and Communication Engineering.

I had already spent considerable time studying subjects such as Analog Electronics, Digital Electronics, Signals and Systems, Control Systems and Communications. That foundation helped me significantly in GATE EC, where I eventually secured AIR 2.

Electrical Engineering, however, has a substantial set of subjects that I had not studied with the same depth.

In particular, Power Systems and Electrical Machines were not areas where I had strong command over the syllabus. I was much more comfortable with the electronics-oriented portions of the paper.

So I did not approach GATE EE as someone who had mastered every subject in the syllabus.

Instead, I tried to understand how much of the paper I could solve using the fundamentals I already possessed.

Building on Existing Fundamentals

One advantage of having prepared seriously for GATE EC earlier is that the underlying engineering fundamentals stay with you.

Circuit analysis, signals, control, mathematics, semiconductor concepts and problem-solving techniques are not isolated to a single GATE paper.

A large part of the preparation was therefore about recognizing these connections and revisiting concepts rather than learning everything from scratch.

For the portions of EE that were familiar, I focused on sharpening problem-solving ability.

For subjects where my preparation was weaker, I concentrated on understanding the fundamental relationships and solving representative problems rather than trying to develop the same depth that I had built for Electronics.

This also meant being honest about what I knew and what I did not.

Problems Over Passive Preparation

As with my earlier GATE preparation, I found problem-solving much more valuable than simply reading theory.

A question often tells you more about your preparation than another hour of passive revision.

When I got something wrong, the important part was understanding why:

Was the underlying concept unclear?

Was there a mathematical mistake?

Did I misread the question?

Or was there simply a pattern that I had not encountered before?

This helped me identify the portions of the syllabus that actually required attention.

The objective was not to become an expert in every topic.

It was to build enough understanding across the paper to consistently convert the questions that were within my reach.

A Different Kind of GATE Result

My AIR 2 in GATE EC and AIR 26 in GATE EE are therefore quite different achievements.

The first came from a much deeper and more deliberate preparation of the Electronics syllabus.

The second came from applying an existing engineering foundation to a paper where several core subjects were outside my strongest areas.

That distinction is probably the most important thing I took away from this attempt.

You do not always need complete coverage to perform well.

You need to understand your strengths, recognize your gaps, and make sure that the fundamentals you do possess are strong enough to extract marks from the paper.

The 2026 Result and the Controversy

GATE 2026 also had an unusual atmosphere around the EE results.

The official result lists Tejavath Manoj Kumar as AIR 1 in Electrical Engineering with 92 marks.

After the results, there was considerable discussion within the GATE community questioning the unusually high rank distribution and the lack of publicly visible information about several top rankers. Some discussions went as far as alleging cheating.

I would be careful about drawing conclusions from those discussions because allegations and online speculation are not the same as established facts.

What was interesting from a candidate’s perspective, however, was seeing how quickly the conversation around ranks shifted from preparation and performance to questions about the credibility of the result.

For someone who had already experienced GATE once at a very high level, it was a reminder that the rank itself is only one part of the story.

You ultimately have control over your own preparation and performance, not over how the rest of the ranking turns out.

What I Take Away

GATE EC AIR 2 taught me what deep preparation can achieve.

GATE EE AIR 26 taught me something slightly different: strong fundamentals can remain useful even when you move outside your strongest specialization.

I did not have the same depth in Power Systems or Electrical Machines that I had in Electronics.

I did not need to pretend otherwise.

Instead, I tried to make the most of the engineering knowledge I had accumulated over the years and solve the paper from that foundation.

That, more than the rank itself, is probably what I find most satisfying about this result.

Fundamentals compound.

You may not remember every formula or every chapter years later, but a strong conceptual foundation gives you something to build on when you encounter a new problem.

And sometimes, that is enough to take you much further than you initially expect.
